GEO, explained: what changes for SEO and what you can do

Being cited by an AI rather than just ranking well on Google: that’s the promise of GEO. Here I bring together what I learned at FePSeM Day, what it changes for SEO, and what you can actually do right now.

By Nada Iddouch · 20 November 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways

  • GEO is about getting your brand into AI answers, with reasons the customer understands. It builds on SEO without replacing it.
  • The major AI bots only read raw HTML: price, stock, specs and reviews need to be in it.
  • What others say about you (reviews, press, comparisons, tests) weighs far more than what you say about yourself.
  • The click is only the tip of the iceberg: you also need to measure mentions, share of voice and cited sources.

Why everyone is talking about GEO

At FePSeM Day in Lille, GEO came up in almost every conversation. I wanted to bring together here what I learned during the day, explain it simply, and above all list what you can actually do.

Context first. 73% of French people say they trust artificial intelligence, 11 points more than a year ago. And almost one Black Friday and Cyber Monday shopper in two plans to use AI to prepare their purchases, compared with one in three the year before. AI is becoming a shopping adviser.

What is GEO?

GEO stands for Generative Engine Optimization. The definition given by David Groult and Even Hochart (NOIISE) is the clearest I’ve heard: getting the brand into AI answers, with reasons the customer understands.

Their example: someone asks an AI, “I’m new to hiking, how do I choose my shoes?”. The AI answers like a shop assistant: a mid-height upper to support the ankle, try them on at the end of the day… GEO is about getting it to add “I’d look at this model”, with a use case (day hikes on marked trails), an argument (lugged sole), a proof (waterproofing measured by an independent lab) and even a limit (a bit heavy for mountain running).

Alongside GEO there is GEA, Generative Engine Advertising: buying a presence clearly labelled as sponsored in those answers. In France, ChatGPT has shown ads since 24 August 2026 and Microsoft Copilot since 2024, while Google is still testing them in its AI answers. It’s a bit like the SEO and paid search duo, moved into assistants.

Where GEO fits in the buying journey

Between the moment a need appears and the purchase, customers go through what Google calls the messy middle: they explore (“what are my options?”, the choice widens) then evaluate (“which one should I pick?”, the choice narrows), often going back and forth. AI steps in exactly there, in both phases.

The problem is that this part is almost invisible. The click is only the tip of the iceberg: according to the figures quoted, around 1% of prompts lead to a click. Discovering, comparing and shortlisting the product happen before, inside the conversation, without showing up in your analytics. So GEO works before and beyond the traffic you can attribute.

GEO and SEO: what stays, what changes

GEO doesn’t replace SEO: it builds on it. A page that bots can’t find, can’t understand or don’t trust has no chance of being cited, whether by Google or by an AI. But several things change:

SEOGEO
GoalRank well in a list of resultsBe cited or recommended in an answer
Who must be able to read the siteGooglebot, BingbotThe same, plus AI bots: training, search, on demand, agents
Reading the pageGoogle can run JavaScriptThe major AI bots tested don’t run JavaScript: only the HTML counts
What builds authorityContent, links, reputationAbove all, what others say about you: reviews, press, comparisons, tests
MeasurementRankings, clicks, trafficMentions, visibility, share of voice, cited sources

The good news is that the foundations stay the same: a page that is technically accessible, clear, that answers a real intent and rests on a reputation. GEO simply takes those principles further, and beyond your own website.

What the numbers show

NOIISE presented a study run with its Acoustiik tool on 2,072 prompts in October 2026, about ChatGPT’s product carousels. As they stressed themselves, it should be read with caution: it’s a snapshot at a given moment, on their clients, while AI changes very fast. But the trends are telling:

  • Buying questions trigger carousels. A product carousel appears in 70.1% of results when the question is about choosing a product, 52.2% when it’s about where to buy, and only 9.2% for an information or care question.
  • AI draws on a whole ecosystem of pages. Among cited sources: 28.9% product pages, 13.6% category pages, 13.0% guides and 44.5% other types. The information doesn’t have to fit on a single page.
  • But the purchase happens on the product page. 84.4% of purchase links lead to a product page.
  • Retailers dominate the shelf. They take up 42.8% of carousels, ahead of direct-to-consumer brands (24.7%), hybrid sites (22.1%) and marketplaces (10.4%).
  • Rankings matter. 37.2% of the prompts analysed cite at least one “top 10 best…” type of article.
  • An AI can recommend a brand without citing its website. For the brand Cookut, 48% of answers naming it don’t cite cookut.com. On one prompt, ChatGPT described one of its casserole dishes using 21 sources, including a Que Choisir comparison.

What you can do right now

1. Let the right bots in

Everything starts with access. It isn’t about blocking or allowing “AI” as a whole, but choosing bot by bot: those used for training, for search, for on-demand visits or for agents. An e-commerce site, for instance, has every reason to let search bots and on-demand visits through. Also check what happens upstream: Cloudflare, the CDN or the firewall can block those bots, or rewrite your robots.txt, without you knowing. I went into detail in my write-up of the day.

2. Serve the essentials in the HTML

According to the tests presented, none of the major AI bots (GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, ChatGPT-User, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot…) runs JavaScript. If price, stock, specs or reviews are loaded with JavaScript, the bot sees an empty page, or just “Loading…”. That information has to be in the HTML sent by the server.

3. Add information gain

The web is saturated with identical pages. The point isn’t to produce more content, but to bring information others don’t have: what the buyer knows after leaving your page that they didn’t know before. To find it, look where customers hesitate: customer service, negative reviews, questions, returns.

Three real examples shown during the session:

  • Back Market doesn’t just say “good condition”: each condition has its own price and markers (casing, battery, original parts). AI can link a visible condition to a price.
  • MaxiCoffee replaces “intense and high quality” with a roast scale from 1 to 10, from acidity to bitterness. AI can compare two coffees on a shared scale.
  • ManoMano shows a “Compare with similar items” table on the product page, in HTML: price, reviews, delivery, volume. AI knows which products to compare, and on what.

4. Get others to confirm it

What you publish about yourself is only a small part of the equation, around 20% according to NOIISE. The rest comes from what others confirm: an independent tester, customer reviews, a media outlet or a comparison. In practice, that means publishing precise, verifiable proof on the page (“waterproof for 8 hours under simulated rain, test method published”), then getting it confirmed elsewhere.

On rankings, the nuance matters. Ranking yourself first without a method is to be avoided: it’s borderline legally and likely to become a negative signal. A ranking based on verifiable criteria, certifications or recognised awards is legitimate. And real success is being a brand strong enough that no serious ranking can leave it out.

5. Take care of the whole page ecosystem

Since AI draws on product pages, categories and guides, and purchases close on the product page, every level counts: the brand must be identifiable, the offer complete (reference, variants, price, delivery, stock) and advice pages genuinely useful. Product feeds are gaining importance too: ChatGPT has opened a Business Hub in beta that lets brands submit their products, with price and stock, and track their mentions.

6. Measure differently

Traffic is no longer enough to judge your visibility. You need to track representative prompts and measure, engine by engine and intent by intent: brand mentions, visibility, share of voice and cited sources. On buying questions, NOIISE tracks three levels: the brand (is it in the selection?), the merchant (who gets the purchase link?) and the product (which reference, which offer?).

7. Test advertising, within a framework

NOIISE shared its first ChatGPT Ads campaign figures, from September to November 2026: an average CPC of €0.64, a CTR of 1.74% and an indicative ROAS of 1.13. In e-commerce, a click costs roughly what it does on Shopping; in lead generation, it would be 2 to 5 times cheaper than on Search. These are early benchmarks, on a format still in testing, not a standard.

Two pieces of advice struck me as very sound. For targeting, describe the audience (“families preparing a first safari, high budget, looking for a reassuring organised trip”) rather than copying prompts or piling up keywords. And for testing: a capped budget, a fixed duration, and a decision written down before you start (stop, extend or scale).

Where to start on Monday

NOIISE’s session ended with a four-step plan that I find perfect for getting started without spreading yourself thin:

  • Pick one category, just one, from your catalogue.
  • Identify one hesitation customers have, using customer service, reviews, questions or returns.
  • Produce a proof that answers it, on the product page, then verifiable by a third party.
  • Measure and test: track how mentions evolve, and possibly run a capped ad test.

What I think

Leaving the day, my main feeling is that GEO doesn’t erase SEO: it takes it seriously. Being accessible, clear and precise, proving what you claim and building a reputation are fundamentals. What really changes is that you have to think beyond your own website, and accept that a large part of the decision happens where you can’t see it.

And what comes next is arriving fast. Payment protocols inside assistants are multiplying, in the United States first. In France, people still buy on the merchant’s site, but tomorrow AI could work like a marketplace. When that happens, price, stock, delivery and returns will become the offer itself. Better to prepare now.

My day at FePSeM Day in Lille

Today I’m at FePSeM Day in Lille, a day dedicated to search marketing. Here’s why I’m going, the questions I’m bringing with me and what I’m already taking away from the morning.

By Nada Iddouch · 20 November 2026 · 7 min read

Key takeaways

  • FePSeM Day brings search marketing professionals together in Lille around SEO, SEA, GEO, AI and e-commerce.
  • I’m going to test my self-taught practice against other professionals’ experience.
  • My big questions right now: showing up in AI answers, e-commerce SEO and the right metrics to track.
  • The morning was a reminder of the basics: before an AI can cite a site, it has to be able to crawl it, and a CDN, Cloudflare or a WAF can block AI bots without you knowing.

Today, off to Lille

This Friday, 20 November, I’m in Lille for FePSeM Day, at Espace Inkermann Châtillon. A full day dedicated to search marketing: talks, case studies and plenty of conversations between practitioners.

FePSeM is the Fédération des Professionnels du Search Marketing, the French federation of search marketing professionals. It brings together people who, like me, spend their days asking how to be found, understood and chosen online. This edition’s programme covers exactly what I’m working on right now: SEO, SEA, GEO, artificial intelligence and e-commerce.

The programme

From 9 am to 5 pm, the day runs through talks and a round table around one common thread: generative AI applied to search marketing and e-commerce. Here are the announced sessions:

  • Opening keynote — Patrick Valibus
  • “Is GEO only the SEO team’s job?” — Mouad Boulaabi (Smartkeyword)
  • “How can you control the content AI systems use?” — Philippe Yonnet (Neper)
  • “AI: the new battleground for e-merchants” — David Groult (Head of SEO, NOIISE) and Even Hochart (Head of Ads, NOIISE)
  • “Built-in AI: how far can you go with AI and MCP?” (2:45 pm) — Christophe Vidal and Patrick Valibus
  • Round table “AI and e-commerce: what major retailers are preparing for 2027” — Hélène Soudant (SEO project lead, Leroy Merlin) and Elise Angot Levin (Traffic Manager Northern Europe, La Redoute)

Why I’m going

I learned SEO on my own, in the evenings, watching videos, then building sites and getting a lot wrong. That path gave me good instincts, but it has a limit: you end up going round in circles inside your own certainties.

A day like this is the opposite. You listen to people working on other sites, other industries, other constraints. You compare. You find out what others are testing before it’s written everywhere.

And the timing matters. Search is changing fast: AI-generated answers, conversational engines, where brands fit in those new answers… The job I learned a few years ago is already not quite the same. I’d rather understand that change by talking to the people living it than just put up with it.

The questions I’m bringing

I’m arriving with a few questions in mind, the ones that come up most often in my work:

  • GEO: how can a brand show up in AI answers, and how do you actually measure it?
  • E-commerce: what still works, and what is changing, when it comes to ranking product and category pages?
  • Data: which metrics should you track when some clicks disappear in favour of direct answers?
  • AI day to day: how do you use it to save time without losing quality or a voice of your own?

How I approach a conference

Over time I’ve picked up a few habits. I don’t try to write everything down: I only keep the ideas I can test in the weeks that follow. A conference is only worth it to me if it turns into action.

I also pay as much attention to the breaks as to the talks. That’s often where the most useful exchanges happen: a remark by the coffee machine, a story shared without slides, a new person to follow.

What I took away: the morning

The morning was really interesting, because it went back to where everything starts: crawling. There’s a lot of talk about GEO and visibility in AI answers, but before content can be cited, it first has to be readable. If a bot can’t access a page, that page simply doesn’t exist for it.

Allowing (or not) AI bots

We looked at why it matters to decide clearly which AI bots you let crawl your site. Each company has its own bots, with their own names, and they don’t all do the same job: some collect content to train models, others fetch information in real time to answer a question.

Knowing those names (for example GPTBot or OAI-SearchBot at OpenAI, ClaudeBot at Anthropic, PerplexityBot, or Google-Extended on Google’s side) lets you make informed choices in your robots.txt, rather than blocking or opening everything by default.

The blocking you don’t see: CDN, Cloudflare and WAF

This is the point that struck me most. You can have a perfectly configured robots.txt and still block AI bots without knowing it. The cause is often upstream of the site:

  • The CDN, which sits between the visitor and the server, can filter some bots before they ever reach the site.
  • Cloudflare, which is very widely used, offers options to block AI bots, and they can be switched on without anyone realising.
  • The WAF (web application firewall), there to protect the site, can mistake a legitimate bot for a threat and block it.

The result: SEO thinks the door is open, while the infrastructure has shut it. It’s exactly the kind of issue you don’t fix alone, but with the technical teams.

What I’m going to do

Back home, I want to check three simple things on the sites I work on: what the robots.txt says about AI bots, the CDN and firewall settings, and the server logs to see which bots actually come through. That’s exactly the kind of idea I like to bring back from a conference: concrete and testable straight away.
